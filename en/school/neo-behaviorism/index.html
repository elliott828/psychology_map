Neo-Behaviorism

School·1932·新行为主义

Keeps the method of objective experiment but admits 'intervening variables' behind behavior: purpose, expectation and drive. Tolman and Hull are the two major figures.

Historical influence ●●●○○·Evidence: Mostly historical·Public familiarity ●○○○○

Thread
Learning and Behavior
Landmark
Tolman's Purposive Behavior in Animals and Men introduces purpose and expectation into behavioral research as internal variables
What the evidence says
Hull's drive-reduction theory has been abandoned; Tolman's cognitive maps and latent learning were later confirmed by further research.
Key figure
Edward Tolman

Core theories

  • Latent Learning 1930Without food rewards, rats seem not to have learned the maze. Once rewards are introduced, they immediately follow the route they already knew. Learning happened, but it was not shown in behavior.
  • Drive Reduction Theory 1943Internal deficits such as hunger and thirst create drives. Behavior serves to reduce those drives, and responses that reduce them get reinforced.
  • Cognitive Map 1948Rats in a maze do not just learn 'turn left, turn right' movements. They form an overall representation of space in the brain, so when a path is blocked, they can take a new route.

Key people

  • Clark Hull 1884–1952
  • Edward Tolman 1886–1959

Key concepts

  • Intervening VariableInternal processes, such as expectations and purposes, that Tolman placed between stimulus and response.

Relations to other schools

  • ← Branches BehaviorismKeeps objective methods but adds internal variables such as purpose and expectation.
  • → Develops Social Learning TheoryTolman's expectancies and internal representations kept cognitive processes in Bandura's theory.
  • → Bridges Cognitive RevolutionTolman's cognitive maps brought internal representations inside behaviorism.
