Hypnosis, Hysteria and Dissociation

School·1889·催眠、癔症与解离

Consciousness is not always unified. In hypnosis, hysteria or after intense trauma, part of experience can split off from consciousness and operate independently.

Historical influence ●●●○○·Evidence: Supported, with limits·Public familiarity ●●●○○

Thread
Unconscious and Depth
Landmark
Janet's Psychological Automatism; Charcot's hysteria research in the 1880s
What the evidence says
Dissociation as a symptom is widely recognized. The causal chain from trauma to dissociation, and the origins of dissociative identity disorder, remain debated.
Key figure
Pierre Janet

Core theories

  • Charcot's Hysteria and Hypnosis 1887Hysteria was not 'faking illness'. It was a real disorder that could be reproduced through hypnosis. Its symptoms came from psychological causes rather than bodily injury.
  • Psychological Automatism 1889Some experiences are 'split off' from the main consciousness and operate like a separate small system. This causes forgetfulness, paralysis, or states that feel like being 'another person'.
  • Dissociative Identity Disorder Debate 1989Whether a person really splits into several 'personalities', or whether this is a performance shaped by suggestion and role expectations, is still debated.

Key people

  • Jean-Martin Charcot 1825–1893
  • Pierre Janet 1859–1947

Key concepts

  • DissociationA separation of functions that normally integrate, such as consciousness, memory and identity.
