Conformity and Obedience

School·1951·从众与服从

Under group pressure and authority, ordinary people act against their own judgment or conscience. These experiments made people aware of the power of the situation.

Historical influence ●●●●○·Evidence: Supported, with limits·Public familiarity ●●●●●

Thread
Society and Culture
Landmark
Asch's line experiment: many people changed their answers in the face of an obviously wrong majority (Milgram, 1963)
What the evidence says
Conformity and obedience experiments have been repeated many times since, but their scale and interpretation need revision. Most people do not obey 'blindly.'
Key figure
Solomon Asch

Core theories

  • Asch Conformity Experiments 1951A group of 'confederates' deliberately gave obviously wrong answers. About a third of the real participants' answers were pulled off course. People will go against their own eyes out of fear of standing out.
  • Milgram Obedience Studies 1963When told to by the experimenter, most participants turned the 'shock' up to very high levels. This shows the power of authority, but they did not obey without any doubt.
  • Bystander Effect 1968The more people around, the less likely any one person is to step in to help. Responsibility is spread out, and people watch one another's reactions.
  • Stanford Prison Experiment 1971Zimbardo claimed that randomly assigned college students quickly became cruel guards. But the archives show the experimenters suggested the behavior, so it is hard to prove the power of the roles themselves.
  • Six Principles of Influence 1984Reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, and scarcity. These six shortcuts make people more likely to say yes.

Key concepts

  • Diffusion of ResponsibilityThe more people present, the less responsible each person feels.
  • Social ProofLooking at what others do to decide what you should do.
  • Foot-in-the-DoorAgreeing to a small request makes it easier to agree to a larger one.
