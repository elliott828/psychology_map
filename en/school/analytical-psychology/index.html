Analytical Psychology

School·1913·分析心理学

Beyond the unconscious left by personal experience, there is a shared human 'collective unconscious,' whose archetypes (mother, hero, shadow) shape dreams and myths. The task of life is individuation: becoming a whole self.

Historical influence ●●●●○·Evidence: Interpretive framework·Public familiarity ●●●●●

Thread
Unconscious and Depth
Landmark
Jung breaks with Freud and adopts the name 'analytical psychology'
What the evidence says
It is a framework that explains the psyche through symbol and myth. 'Archetypes' and the 'collective unconscious' are hard to test experimentally, and the correspondence between its typology and the Five-Factor model is only partial.
Key figure
Carl Gustav Jung

Core theories

  • Word Association and Complexes 1906When certain words touch emotional issues, people respond more slowly or cannot answer. Jung called these clusters of emotionally charged memories 'complexes'. Around 1907, Jung also used a galvanometer to record changes in skin conductance when subjects heard stimulus words, measuring emotional reactions this way.
  • Jung's Model of the Psyche 1912The psyche includes the conscious ego, the personal unconscious (which contains complexes), and the collective unconscious (which contains archetypes). The task of life is individuation.
  • Active Imagination 1916While awake, the person lets inner images arise on their own, then engages with them through painting, writing, or dialogue. This brings unconscious content into awareness.
  • Collective Unconscious and Archetypes 1919Humans share an innate psychological structure, which appears as recurring images such as the mother, the hero, the wise old man, and the shadow.
  • Psychological Types 1921People have two attitudes, introversion and extraversion, and four functions: thinking, feeling, sensation, and intuition. Each person leans toward one or two of them. The MBTI was built on this framework.
  • Stages of Life and the Midlife Transition 1930The first half of life is spent building a career and adapting to the outer world. After midlife, the task turns inward, toward finding life's meaning and moving toward individuation.
  • Synchronicity 1952Two events with no causal link occur together because of their meaning. For example, you dream of someone and that same day you receive a letter from them.

Key concepts

  • ArchetypeTypical figures that recur in dreams and myths.
  • ShadowThe side of yourself you refuse to admit.
  • PersonaThe role you present to others in society.
  • Anima and AnimusThe image of the feminine in a man's psyche, and of the masculine in a woman's.
  • IndividuationThe process of becoming a whole, unique self.
  • ComplexA cluster of unconscious feelings and memories built around an emotional experience.
  • Introversion and ExtraversionWhether energy is directed more inward or toward the outside world.

Relations to other schools

  • ← Branches Classical PsychoanalysisJung rejected the purely sexual reading of libido and broke with Freud.
  • → Develops Post-Jungian and Archetypal PsychologyPost-Jungian scholars carry on and revise Jung's theories.
  • ← Bridges Hypnosis, Hysteria and DissociationJung's concept of the complex drew on Janet's idea of dissociation.
  • → Bridges Hierarchy of Needs and Self-ActualizationThe ideas of individuation and self-actualization are related, though with no direct teacher-student line.
  • → Applies Everyday Self-HelpJung's advice to Hazzard, relayed by Wilson, became one of the sources of the self-help fellowship.
